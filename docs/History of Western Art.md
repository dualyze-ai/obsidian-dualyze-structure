# History of Western Art

A survey of Western art from antiquity to the present day — covering major movements, defining works, and the ideas that shaped each era.

---

## Ancient and Classical

### Greece

Greek artists pursued an ideal of human beauty grounded in mathematics. The Parthenon frieze and the sculpture of Polykleitos both demonstrate the *Canon* — a precise system of proportions for depicting the human figure. Sculpture moved from the rigid Archaic kouros through the Classical ideal toward the psychological expressiveness of the Hellenistic period.

The shift from flat, frontal figures to dynamic, three-dimensional forms happened over roughly three centuries. By the time of the Laocoön group (c. 40 BCE), Greek sculptors could render agony, tension, and narrative drama in marble.

### Rome

Rome absorbed Greek artistic language and redirected it toward civic and imperial ends. Portrait busts captured specific physiognomies with unflinching realism — a deliberate contrast to Greek idealization. The Column of Trajan unrolls 200 meters of continuous narrative relief, functioning as both monument and historical document.

Roman wall painting survives primarily at Pompeii and Herculaneum. The four Pompeian styles range from simple marble imitation to elaborate theatrical illusion, with trompe-l'œil architectural vistas that anticipate Renaissance perspective by fifteen centuries.

## Medieval and Byzantine

### Byzantine Art

Byzantine art rejected Classical naturalism in favor of spiritual transcendence. Gold backgrounds dissolved earthly space; flattened figures turned toward the viewer with large, direct eyes. The image was not a window into a scene but a presence — an icon.

Mosaic was the supreme medium. At Ravenna's San Vitale (c. 547 CE), Emperor Justinian and Empress Theodora appear in solemn procession, their feet barely touching the ground, haloed and frontal. Hierarchy of scale — larger figures are more important — replaced realistic proportion.

### Romanesque and Gothic

Romanesque churches used art as scripture for the illiterate. Portal tympana depicted the Last Judgment in compressed, urgent relief. Inside, narrative cycles in fresco moved around the walls like illustrated texts.

Gothic cathedrals transformed this program through light. As stone structure was refined into slender piers and ribbed vaults, walls dissolved into stained glass. At Chartres, over 150 windows filter colored light into the nave. The figures became slightly more naturalistic — more weight, more drapery movement — anticipating what came next.

## Renaissance

### Early Renaissance (15th Century)

The Renaissance did not discover perspective — it systematized it. Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated linear perspective around 1420 with a painted panel and a mirror. Leon Battista Alberti codified it in *On Painting* (1435). Suddenly every painter had a tool for constructing convincing pictorial space.

Masaccio's *Trinity* fresco (c. 1427) deploys the new perspective with startling confidence — the painted barrel vault recedes so convincingly that contemporaries described it as a hole in the wall. Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, and Fra Angelico each absorbed perspective while maintaining distinctive approaches to line, color, and spiritual feeling.

### High Renaissance (c. 1490–1520)

Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael arrived at a synthesis that defined the High Renaissance ideal: harmonious composition, idealised figures, and the capacity to express psychological depth through pose and gesture.

Leonardo's *sfumato* — smoky transitions between tone — replaced the crisp outlines of Early Renaissance painting with something more atmospheric and suggestive. Raphael's *School of Athens* organized fifty figures across a vast architectural space with complete clarity. Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling contained over 300 figures in a unified theological programme.

### Northern Renaissance

North of the Alps, the Renaissance took a different form. Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden developed oil paint into a medium of almost supernatural precision — fur, jewels, reflected light in eyes. Rather than monumental figures in open space, northern painting favored intimate interiors, accumulated detail, and an attention to the textures of ordinary life.

Dürer consciously bridged north and south, traveling to Italy and returning to Germany with Italian perspective and proportion absorbed into his own meticulous draftsmanship.

## Baroque and Rococo

### Baroque

The Baroque emerged from the Counter-Reformation's demand for art that moved the faithful. Caravaggio achieved this through tenebrism — figures dramatically lit against near-total darkness — and through radical earthiness: saints with dirty feet, apostles with weather-beaten hands.

Rembrandt used light differently, building it from within the paint surface through layers of glaze. His portraits dissolve the boundary between subject and viewer; his self-portraits constitute one of the most sustained meditations on identity in all of Western art.

Rubens represented the Baroque at its most exuberant — large formats, dynamic composition, warm flesh tones, figures in motion. Velázquez achieved something rarer: *Las Meninas* (1656) stages a visual puzzle about representation, the act of painting, and the viewer's position that still generates argument.

### Rococo

Rococo grew from the Baroque but shed its religious gravity. Watteau invented the *fête galante* — elegantly dressed figures in parkland settings, drifting through ambiguous pleasures. Fragonard and Boucher filled aristocratic interiors with pink and gold fantasies.

The style was associated with French court culture and fell into disrepute after the Revolution as a symbol of aristocratic frivolity. Neoclassicism rose partly as a moral corrective.

## Impressionism and Post-Impressionism

### Impressionism

In 1874, a group of painters rejected by the official Salon organized their own exhibition. Critics mocked Claude Monet's loosely brushed *Impression, Sunrise* — the name stuck. Impressionism committed to painting light as experienced, not as known. Shadows were colored, not brown. Edges dissolved. Paint strokes became visible.

The Impressionists painted outdoors, worked quickly, and returned to the same subjects repeatedly to catch different light conditions. Monet's series paintings — haystacks, Rouen Cathedral, the Thames — are essentially scientific studies of how illumination transforms form.

Renoir brought the same sensibility to human subjects: dappled light on skin, the blur of a crowd, the pleasure of an afternoon. Degas, more detached, studied movement — dancers, bathers, jockeys — with the cool eye of someone arranging forms.

### Post-Impressionism

Post-Impressionism is less a movement than a convergence of four very different responses to Impressionism's legacy.

- **Cézanne** wanted to restore structure to painting without abandoning Impressionism's color discoveries. His geometric simplifications of form directly influenced Cubism.
- **Seurat** took Impressionist color theory and systematized it into Pointillism — every dot of paint a calculated optical mixture.
- **Van Gogh** charged brushwork with psychological urgency. Line and color became vehicles for emotional states rather than descriptions of appearances.
- **Gauguin** sought a spiritually charged simplicity, first in Brittany and then in Tahiti — bold contours, flat planes, non-naturalistic color.

## Modern and Contemporary

### Early Modernism

The twentieth century's first decades produced a cascade of movements, each rejecting its predecessor.

Fauvism (Matisse, Derain) pushed color free of descriptive function. Cubism (Picasso, Braque) fractured single-point perspective into multiple simultaneous viewpoints. Futurism celebrated speed and technology. Expressionism (Kirchner, Munch) prioritized emotional intensity over visual accuracy. Abstract art (Kandinsky, Mondrian) abandoned representation altogether.

Each movement proposed a different answer to the same question: if photography can record appearances, what is painting for?

### Abstract Expressionism and After

After World War II, the center of the art world shifted from Paris to New York. Abstract Expressionism — Pollock, de Kooning, Rothko, Kline — made scale and gesture central. Pollock dripped paint from above; Rothko built luminous color fields; de Kooning returned to the figure through violent brushwork.

Pop Art (Warhol, Lichtenstein) turned the tables on high-art pretension by embracing commercial imagery. Minimalism (Judd, Flavin) stripped art down to pure object, pure material, pure light.

Contemporary art after the 1970s is characterized by plurality rather than succession: conceptual practice, installation, video, performance, digital media, and painting all coexist without a dominant movement or ideology. Artists work across media, across cultures, and in explicit dialogue with both art history and the broader world.
