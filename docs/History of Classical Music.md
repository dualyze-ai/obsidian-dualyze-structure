# History of Classical Music

A survey of Western classical music from the Baroque era to the present — covering the defining forms, techniques, and ideas of each period.

---

## Baroque Era

### Counterpoint

Counterpoint is the art of combining independent melodic lines so that they sound simultaneously coherent and individually distinct. Bach brought counterpoint to its highest refinement in the *Well-Tempered Clavier* and the *Art of Fugue* — two works that exhausted the possibilities of the form rather than simply exemplifying them.

The fugue is counterpoint's most demanding structure. A theme (subject) is stated in one voice, then answered by a second voice while the first continues with new material (countersubject), then taken up by a third. The architecture is mathematical, yet Bach's fugues never sound mechanical: the logic and the lyricism are inseparable.

### Harmony and Tonality

The Baroque period established the tonal system that dominated Western music until the early twentieth century. Major and minor keys replaced the earlier system of modes; chords acquired directional function; the listener developed an expectation of resolution that composers could fulfill, delay, or deny.

The figured bass — a shorthand notation indicating harmonies above a written bass line — was the practical expression of this new harmonic thinking. Keyboard players improvised upper voices from these figures, making harmony a real-time creative act rather than a fully notated prescription.

### Ornamentation

Baroque performance was inseparable from ornamentation. Trills, turns, mordents, appoggiaturas — these small decorative figures were not merely cosmetic; they articulated rhythm, emphasized dissonances, and gave shape to phrases. Many were not notated but understood: a competent performer knew when and how to add them.

Handel's vocal writing and Couperin's harpsichord music represent opposite poles of Baroque ornamental practice. Handel's ornamentation served drama and expression; Couperin's was architectural — each piece fitted together from precisely placed ornamental details like a piece of marquetry.

## Classical Period

### Sonata Form

Sonata form is the most important structural principle in Western instrumental music. It organizes a movement into three broad sections: an Exposition (two contrasting themes established in related keys), a Development (themes fragmented, combined, and put under harmonic pressure), and a Recapitulation (themes returned, now both in the home key).

The form's power lies in the tension between its predictability and its flexibility. Audiences expected the return of the home key; composers found endless ways to defer, surprise, or subvert that expectation. Haydn specialized in structural humor — false recapitulations, premature endings — while Mozart used the form to build psychological depth.

### The Symphony

The symphony — a multi-movement orchestral work — became the prestige genre of the Classical period. Haydn wrote 104; Mozart 41; Beethoven only 9, but each was a structural and emotional landmark.

The Classical orchestra was a precision instrument: strings in four parts, pairs of woodwinds (flute, oboe, bassoon, later clarinet), pairs of horns and trumpets, and timpani. Beethoven expanded this ensemble and its dynamic range, writing *fortissimo* passages that pushed the physical limits of the period's instruments and the expressive expectations of its audiences.

### Chamber Music

The string quartet — two violins, viola, and cello — was the intimate counterpart of the symphony. Haydn invented the mature form of the genre; his Op. 33 quartets introduced a new equality between the four voices that replaced the earlier texture of a decorated melody over accompaniment.

Mozart's six quartets dedicated to Haydn are among the most technically demanding works ever written for the medium. Beethoven's late quartets (Op. 127–135) are perhaps the most profound: they break sonata form from within, introducing fugue, variation, and recitative into a genre previously defined by elegant balance.

## Romantic Era

### Programme Music

Programme music is instrumental music explicitly linked to a narrative, image, or idea. Berlioz's *Symphonie Fantastique* (1830) told a specific autobiographical story; its *idée fixe* — a recurring theme representing the beloved — was a new kind of musical symbol, transforming over the work's five movements as the protagonist's obsession deepens into horror.

Liszt developed the symphonic poem: a single-movement orchestral work shaped by a literary or visual programme. Richard Strauss brought the form to its apogee with *Don Quixote* and *Ein Heldenleben* — works of almost cinematic descriptive power.

### Nationalism

The Romantic period coincided with the rise of nationalist movements across Europe. Composers drew on folk melody, dance rhythm, and local legend to create music that expressed national identity.

Smetana's *Má vlast* conjured the rivers and forests of Bohemia; Sibelius's *Finlandia* became a political act as much as a musical one; Bartók and Kodály collected thousands of genuine folk songs and allowed their irregular rhythms and modal harmonies to reshape their compositional language at its roots.

### Virtuosity

The Romantic period elevated the performing soloist to a new kind of cultural hero. Paganini played the violin at the edge of what seemed humanly possible; rumors circulated that he had made a pact with the devil. Liszt transformed the piano recital into a theatrical event, placing the instrument sideways to show his profile and playing from memory — then a radical gesture.

Chopin directed virtuosity inward rather than outward. His études are technical exercises that are simultaneously profound compositions; his nocturnes and ballades make demands of a different kind — on the performer's imagination and sense of color rather than mere digital dexterity.

## Modernism

### Atonality

By 1908, Schoenberg had dissolved the tonal system that had organized Western music for two centuries. *Three Piano Pieces* Op. 11 and *Pierrot Lunaire* (1912) moved through harmonic space without the gravitational pull of a home key. Every note was theoretically equal; no chord implied direction.

This free atonality created intense expressive compression — the short, dense forms of Webern's Five Pieces for Orchestra last seconds rather than minutes — but it also removed the large-scale architecture that tonality had made possible. The tension between atonal freedom and structural coherence defined the next fifty years of new music.

### Serialism

To provide order without tonality, Schoenberg developed the twelve-tone method: a fixed ordering of all twelve chromatic pitches (the *row*) from which all melodic and harmonic material was derived. The row could be used in its original form, inverted, retrograded, or retrograde-inverted — forty-eight possible transformations, each preserving the interval relationships.

Webern took serialism further than Schoenberg, applying the principle not just to pitch but to rhythm, dynamics, and articulation. This *total serialism* was taken up by Boulez, Stockhausen, and Nono after 1945 — producing music of extraordinary structural rigour and, critics argued, correspondingly limited human appeal.

### Neo-Classicism

The other major response to the collapse of tonality was not forward into atonality but backward to earlier models. Stravinsky's neo-classical works — *Pulcinella*, the *Symphony of Psalms*, the *Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments* — adopted the forms and textures of Baroque and Classical music while distorting them with wrong-note harmonies and irregular rhythms.

Prokofiev pursued a parallel path: tonal but astringent, melodically clear but harmonically unexpected. The neo-classical aesthetic valued wit, objectivity, and craft over Romantic self-expression. It was, Schoenberg said dismissively, a return to the past — a charge Stravinsky accepted with equanimity.

## After 1945

### Minimalism

Minimalism emerged in New York in the 1960s as a reaction against both serialism's complexity and modernism's difficulty. Philip Glass, Steve Reich, Terry Riley, and La Monte Young built music from simple melodic or rhythmic cells repeated and slowly transformed over long time spans.

Reich's *Music for 18 Musicians* (1976) moves through a cycle of eleven chords, each sustained for the length of a breath; within this slow harmonic motion, interlocking patterns create a shimmering, constantly shifting texture. Glass's early operas — *Einstein on the Beach*, *Satyagraha* — applied the same principle to music theater: time expanded to the point where ordinary narrative expectations dissolved.

### Electronic Music

The tape recorder and the oscillator gave composers direct access to sound without performers. Pierre Schaeffer's *musique concrète* assembled recordings of real-world sounds into abstract compositions; Stockhausen's *Gesang der Jünglinge* transformed a boy's singing voice into a spatial sonic environment distributed across five loudspeakers.

The synthesizer made electronic music accessible to rock musicians and composers alike. By the 1970s, the distinction between classical and popular electronic music was already porous. Morton Subotnick's *Silver Apples of the Moon*, Wendy Carlos's *Switched-On Bach*, and Kraftwerk's *Autobahn* were made on related instruments and addressed overlapping audiences.

### Spectralism

Spectral music, developed in Paris in the 1970s by Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail, derives its pitch and rhythm materials from the acoustic properties of sound itself — specifically, from the harmonic spectrum produced when a physical object vibrates. A low C on the cello produces not just a fundamental pitch but a series of overtones; spectral composers used this series as the basis for harmony.

The result is music that sounds natural and organic in an unfamiliar way: the harmonics feel inevitable, yet the overall language is unlike anything in the tonal or serial tradition. Grisey's *Partiels* (1975) opens with a single bass trombone note and slowly unfolds its spectrum across the ensemble over twenty minutes — as if the physics of sound were itself a form of architecture.
