# Jazz Music Guide

An introduction to jazz — its history, harmonic language, and the art of improvisation — for listeners and players approaching the music for the first time.

---

## Origins and History

### New Orleans

Jazz emerged in New Orleans at the turn of the twentieth century from a confluence of African American musical traditions: blues, ragtime, the spiritual, field hollers, and the marching band music of Congo Square. The city's unique cultural mixture — French, Spanish, African, Creole, and Caribbean influences — produced a social environment where these streams could cross-pollinate.

The earliest jazz was collective improvisation: trumpet, clarinet, and trombone played simultaneously over a rhythm section, each voice weaving around the others. The front-line instruments improvised together without a written part between them. Listening to Louis Armstrong's Hot Five recordings from 1925–1928 is the fastest way to understand what this sounded like — and how quickly one player of genius could transform the idiom.

### The Swing Era

In the 1930s, jazz became popular music. Big bands of fifteen to twenty players replaced the small New Orleans combo. Precise written arrangements gave bandleaders like Duke Ellington and Count Basie control over texture and dynamics that small-group improvisation could not achieve. Ellington in particular composed extended works — *Black, Brown and Beige*, *Such Sweet Thunder* — that challenged the assumption that jazz was purely an improvising music.

The soloist remained central. Lester Young's floating, horizontal lines over Basie's band changed what the tenor saxophone could be. Coleman Hawkins had defined the instrument as thick and chordal; Young made it cool and melodic.

### Bebop and After

Bebop in the early 1940s was a deliberate challenge to commercial swing. Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie played faster, used more complex harmonies, and made the music difficult to dance to. The tempos were punishing; the chord changes required a different kind of ear training. Jazz became music for listening.

Subsequent decades produced an extraordinary range of responses to bebop's achievement:

- **Cool jazz** (Miles Davis, *Birth of the Cool*, 1949): lighter textures, arranged counterpoint, European chamber influence
- **Hard bop** (Art Blakey, Clifford Brown): blues and gospel reintegrated into bebop's sophistication
- **Modal jazz** (Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue*, 1959): slower harmonic rhythm, improvisation over scales rather than chord changes
- **Free jazz** (Ornette Coleman, Cecil Taylor): abandonment of standard harmony, meter, and form

## Core Instruments

### Rhythm Section

The rhythm section is the engine of a jazz ensemble. Its three components — bass, drums, piano (or guitar) — share the responsibility of keeping time and outlining the harmony, but jazz rhythm sections do not simply keep time mechanically: they interact, respond, and push soloists.

**Bass**: The double bass (or, later, electric bass) walks — plays four beats per bar, outlining the chord root and guiding the harmony. A great bassist like Paul Chambers or Ron Carter does more than this: they create melodic counterlines, anticipate changes, and pull the ensemble forward.

**Drums**: Jazz drumming evolved from the New Orleans parade tradition into an increasingly interactive, conversational role. Max Roach and Art Blakey codified the bebop drumming vocabulary: ride cymbal carries the beat, snare and bass drum punctuate and respond. Elvin Jones (in Coltrane's quartet) fractured the pulse across all four limbs, creating a polyrhythmic web.

**Piano**: In the traditional role, the pianist comps — plays punctuating chords behind soloists. But jazz piano encompasses everything from the orchestral density of Art Tatum to the minimal, spare voicings of Bill Evans. Evans introduced a new way of hearing chord color: his close, resonant voicings influenced every pianist who came after.

### Horn Players

Trumpet and saxophone defined jazz's front line for most of the music's history. **Louis Armstrong** established the trumpet as a vehicle for lyrical improvisation and tonal authority. **Miles Davis** reinvented the instrument with every decade: cool, modal, electric fusion, and finally hip-hop-influenced abstraction. **Chet Baker** brought a fragile, internalized lyricism that operated at the opposite emotional extreme from Armstrong's extroversion.

Saxophone arrived slightly later. **Charlie Parker** made the alto saxophone the defining bebop voice, playing at tempos and with a harmonic intelligence that seemed physically impossible. **John Coltrane** moved from bebop through modal jazz to *A Love Supreme* (1965) — a four-part suite of extraordinary intensity — and then to the edges of free playing. **Sonny Rollins** constructed his improvisations like motivic development in classical music, returning to small fragments and transforming them over the course of a solo.

## Harmony and Theory

### The ii–V–I

The most important harmonic progression in jazz is the ii–V–I: a minor seventh chord, a dominant seventh chord, and a major (or minor) seventh chord, each a fourth apart. In the key of C major: Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7.

This progression generates forward motion because each chord pulls toward the next by a strong interval (a perfect fourth), and the dominant seventh chord contains a tritone — the most harmonically tense interval — that resolves into the major seventh.

Jazz musicians learn to recognize ii–V–I patterns in any key instantly, because they appear everywhere. A standard like *Autumn Leaves* is essentially a sequence of ii–V–I progressions in two related keys.

### Chord Extensions and Alterations

Where classical harmony tends to use triads and seventh chords, jazz extends harmonies upward to the ninth, eleventh, and thirteenth. A G13 chord piles four thirds on top of a G triad. These extensions add color and complexity without changing the function of the chord.

Alterations change the quality of specific notes: a G7(♭9♯11) flattens the ninth and raises the eleventh, creating a deliberately harsh color. Jazz players learn to hear these colors as emotional textures — certain voicings suggest intimacy, others confrontation, others ambiguity.

### Standards and the Great American Songbook

Much of jazz is built on the Great American Songbook — popular songs from Broadway and Hollywood musicals written between roughly 1920 and 1960. Songs by Cole Porter, George Gershwin, Rodgers and Hart, Harold Arlen, and Jerome Kern gave jazz musicians a shared harmonic vocabulary and a set of memorable melodies to build from.

A jazz performance of a standard typically states the melody (*head*), improvises over the chord changes (*choruses*), and restates the melody. The challenge is to use the original song as a springboard rather than a constraint — to play something that reveals new meaning in familiar material.

## Improvisation

### Playing the Changes

*Playing the changes* means improvising melodic lines that reflect the underlying chord progression at every moment. On a fast ii–V–I, the improviser has perhaps two beats per chord to play notes that identify the chord quality, lead convincingly to the next chord, and simultaneously constitute a melodic phrase.

The paradox of bebop improvisation is that it sounds spontaneous but requires deep automaticity. Parker could negotiate complex chord changes at 300 beats per minute because he had internalized thousands of melodic patterns — *licks* and *vocabulary* — that were available to him without conscious thought. Improvisation is not making things up from nothing; it is recombining a learned vocabulary in real time.

### Motivic Development

The alternative to running chord tones is motivic improvisation: taking a short phrase — a rhythmic idea, an interval, a melodic contour — and developing it through repetition, transformation, and contrast. Sonny Rollins was the master of this approach; his solo on *Blue 7* (1956) is often cited as a textbook of motivic development.

Miles Davis in his 1960s quintet went further: the band developed *time, no changes* playing, in which the harmonic reference points became loose, and the rhythmic grid elastic. Improvisation here was as much about texture and space as about note choices.

### Listening as a Player

Jazz improvisation is a conversation, not a monologue. The soloist listens to the rhythm section; the rhythm section responds to the soloist; everyone listens to everyone else. Bill Evans described it as *conversational improvisation* — the musical equivalent of talking with someone, where what you say next depends entirely on what they just said.

This is why live jazz, with its genuine risks and real-time responses, often exceeds the recorded versions. The tension of not knowing what comes next — for players and listeners alike — is part of the music's essential nature.

## Essential Listening

A path into the music through ten recordings:

| Album | Artist | Year | Why listen |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Louis Armstrong and His Hot Fives* | Louis Armstrong | 1925–28 | The invention of jazz solo improvisation |
| *Kind of Blue* | Miles Davis | 1959 | The most accessible entry point; modal jazz at its peak |
| *A Love Supreme* | John Coltrane | 1965 | Spiritual intensity and formal ambition |
| *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady* | Charles Mingus | 1963 | Jazz extended composition at its most complex |
| *Song for My Father* | Horace Silver | 1964 | Hard bop at its most inviting |
| *The Bridge* | Sonny Rollins | 1962 | Motivic improvisation, post-bebop |
| *Portrait in Jazz* | Bill Evans Trio | 1959 | The piano trio redefined |
| *Maiden Voyage* | Herbie Hancock | 1965 | Modal jazz with programmatic narrative |
| *Mingus Ah Um* | Charles Mingus | 1959 | Blues, gospel, bebop synthesized |
| *We Insist!* | Max Roach | 1960 | Jazz as political statement |
